# Transactions, saved filters and templates: decisions

Related: feature page [Transactions](../features/transactions.md); architecture [Transactions, imports and receipts](../architecture/transactions.md).

## Current

Both are per-browser, in their own TanStack DB local-storage collections; no table, no endpoint, no backup coverage. A saved filter is the filter half of the ledger search params under a name, applied through the router so the back button still works, and one that names a deleted category, account or tag is applied as stored and marked in the menu. A template is a transaction's shape without its date; Duplicate is the same thing taken from a row, with today's date. Both fill the ordinary create form and write nothing until it is submitted

A refund is an expense with a negative amount, the same signed number in the database and the API, so every expense total nets it and the checks that look for charges skip it with `Amount > 0`. It may name one visible purchase through `refundOfTransactionId`, is not capped at that purchase's amount, cannot be split, and "Record refund" fills the create form from the purchase with its own description. The ledger's type filter has no Refunds value.

A transaction can be spread over 2 to 36 months. Its monthly slices are computed on read from the row (`SpreadSlices`), cut on the payment's day of each month from the payment's month on, or, for a bill paid in arrears, in the months up to and including it, with a largest-remainder split, and located by a stored `SpreadFrom` and `SpreadUntil`; a split's monthly slice is divided across its lines, and a refund is cut by its size into slices that keep its minus. Spending figures count the slices, while the ledger, balances and the other readers of the money that moved see the whole row. A figure's link reaches the spread rows through `spreadOverlap`, which lives only in the URL.

A ledger selection can be deleted with one confirmation and one Undo, one trash entry per row, and moved to another account the caller can see, changing only `AccountId`; a conversion fee, a household split whose payer does not own the account and a shared debt payment on an account outside its household stay where they are and are reported with their codes. Both are browser-only and write one activity row each.

Split lines keep the order they were entered in through a stored `TransactionLine.Position`, set from the request's line index; every read orders a transaction's lines by `Position` then `Id`.

Two rows on one account with the same type, amount and currency, the same stored payee key (or trimmed description when either has none) and dates at most three days apart are possible duplicates, found on read by `PossibleDuplicates.Pairs` and shown through the `duplicates` ledger filter and the month-end checklist. Refunds and two rows imported by one confirm never pair. "Keep both" stores the pair in `DuplicateDismissals`, which follows the rows' visibility; "Delete one" is the ordinary delete.

## Log

Newest first. Each entry is a choice between real alternatives: what was chosen, what was rejected, and why.

- **2026-10-02.** At the owner's request a split and a refund can be spread. A split row is sliced as a whole and each month's slice is divided across its lines with the proration a whole split row gets on its date; a refund is cut by the size of its amount and its slices take its minus back, so the leftover cents go to the earliest months either way. `transaction.spreadRefund` is removed, and Record refund still does not copy the purchase's spread. The cut, the order of slicing and dividing, and Record refund were decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: Prorating each line first and slicing each line's share; handing a negative total to `ShareAllocator`, which puts the larger cents on the last months; copying the purchase's spread into Record refund; keeping `transaction.spreadRefund` published unused
  - Why: Slicing the row first keeps one slice per month for the totals, the trend and the tag, payee and place breakdowns, which then need no lines, and dividing that slice across the lines makes a month's categories add up to its total to the cent, which slicing each line apart would miss by a cent here and there. The slice loader stays a static extension on the visible query, and only `CategoryAttributionService`, which already reads split lines, reads them for spread rows. Cutting the size and restoring the sign gives a refund the same slices as a purchase of the same size, which the ledger's tooltip repeats in `lib/spread-slices.ts`. A refund often covers only part of what was paid or comes back months later, so its spread is the member's own choice rather than the purchase's
- **2026-10-02.** At the owner's request a spread can end in the payment's month, for a bill paid in arrears. `SpreadDirection` is `Forward` (the default, from the payment's month on) or `Backward` (the months up to and including it), an integer column beside `SpreadMonths`, and both ends of the slices are stored, as `SpreadFrom` and `SpreadUntil`, set by `AppDbContext.ApplyEntityRules`. Spread rows written before get `SpreadFrom` from a startup step, `SpreadFromBackfill`. A recurring entry carries the direction too, and the CSV keeps only the months. The storage, the recurring entry and the CSV were decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: A signed `SpreadMonths`, negative for a backward spread; a centred spread; a `SpreadFrom` generated by PostgreSQL; reading `SpreadFrom ?? Date` in every query instead of filling the old rows; a backfill in the migration; leaving recurring entries forward only; a `Spread direction` column in the CSV
  - Why: A sign on the months would make every reader and the check constraint decode it, and the API would have to explain a negative count of months, while an enum names the choice and leaves room for another. With both ends stored, "does this row reach into the window" stays one comparison for the loader, the ledger's drill-through and the month close, whichever way the row is spread. A generated column cannot be written by the backup restore and the member import, which insert every column. Generated migrations are not edited by hand any more, and `PayeeKeyBackfill` and `DebtBalanceBackfill` are the precedent for filling rows at startup. A bill paid in arrears, such as a quarterly utility bill, is most often a recurring entry, so the entry that writes it needs the same choice. The CSV header is shared with the member export and checked by tests, and the months alone still say how far a row is spread
- **2026-10-01.** A selection is moved with `POST /api/transactions/bulk-account`, which changes `AccountId` and nothing else, works per row and refuses three rows with a code in `refused` rather than failing the request: the fee of a currency conversion (the new `transaction.conversionFee`), a household split whose payer does not own the account (`settleUp.notPayer`) and a payment of a shared debt on an account outside its household (`household.referenceNotShared`). Imported rows, split rows, refunds linked to a purchase on another account and rows in another currency move like any other, with no revaluation; rows already on the account are not counted. Decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: Running each row through the full `PUT` with its own body; refusing the whole request when one row cannot move; refusing imported rows because their reference was matched on the old account; refusing rows whose currency differs from the account's; refusing split rows and linked refunds; leaving the conversion fee, split and debt rules to break silently, as the transaction form still lets them
  - Why: The form's account change already keeps the amount, currency, date and therefore the reporting amount, so a move needs no valuation and an account holds any currency as a balance of its own. A selection of fifty rows with one conversion fee in it should move the forty-nine and say why the fiftieth stayed, which is what the toast's warning does. The import reference travelling with the row is what fixes a statement imported into the wrong account: the right account then sees the line as a duplicate, while refusing it would leave no way to correct that mistake short of deleting and importing again. The three refused links are the rules those links are created under, and a move that broke them would leave a fee on an account its conversion is not on, a household split paid from someone else's account, or a shared debt paid from a personal one
- **2026-10-01.** Split rows and the members of an expanded group still cannot be ticked in the ledger, so the new Move to account and Delete act on the same rows as Set category and Set tags; the two endpoints accept split rows and group members like any other row. Decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: Making split rows selectable now and disabling only Set category for a selection that holds one; ticking group members inside an expanded group
  - Why: Changing what can be ticked changes every bulk action at once, and the category rule that keeps split rows out would then need a second, per-action explanation in the toolbar. The API has no reason to refuse them, because a single delete or account change accepts both, so a script or a later change of the selection gets them for free
- **2026-10-01.** Deleting and moving a selection, and undoing a deleted selection, are browser-only: none of `POST /api/transactions/bulk-delete`, `POST /api/transactions/bulk-account` and `POST /api/trash/restore-transactions` carries `TokenWritable`. On shared accounts each writes one summarising activity-log row through `AuditTrail.Summarise`. Decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: Adding them to the token write list beside `bulk-category` and `bulk-tags`; one activity row per transaction
  - Why: A token may already delete one row at a time, which is what an automation that recorded something wrong needs; 200 rows deleted or moved at once is a reshaping of the books a leaked token should not be able to do, and the undo lives in the Trash, which tokens never reach. One row per operation is what every other bulk write already does, so a selection of 200 does not bury the log
- **2026-10-01.** Possible duplicates are pairs on one account with the same type, amount and currency, the same stored `PayeeKey` or, when either key is empty, the same trimmed description, dated at most three days apart; refunds (amount zero or below) and two rows imported by one confirm, recognised by their equal `CreatedAt`, never pair; split rows pair by their total. Decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: A wider window of five or seven days; matching on the description alone or on the reporting amount; leaving split rows out; telling rows of one statement apart by an import batch id, which no row stores
  - Why: Three days is the window the import's hand-entered match already uses for the same question, so both features agree on what "the same payment" means. The payee key is what the reports, rules and unusual amounts already compare, and the description is only the fallback for rows the key normalizes to nothing. A hand-typed split and the bank's unsplit row are exactly the duplicate to catch. One confirm saves all its rows in one `SaveChangesAsync`, which stamps one `CreatedAt`, so two equal coffees listed by the bank stay quiet without a new column
- **2026-10-01.** "Keep both" is a `DuplicateDismissals` table keyed by the two transaction ids with cascading foreign keys, a plain class without owner, timestamps or query filter, so a kept pair follows the rows' visibility; the row action keeps every current pair of the row it is chosen on, and is offered only while the `duplicates` filter is on. Decided while the owner was away, to be reviewed
  - Rejected: A personal dismissal per member like `SubscriptionDismissals`; a flag on the transaction; a pair id in `TransactionResponse` so each row could name its partner; a feature switch
  - Why: Whether two rows are one payment is a fact about the rows, not one person's opinion, so a household partner should not be asked again about a pair the other already answered, and `TransactionTag`'s shape gives that for free: the query only reads a pair beside two rows it can already see. A flag on one row could not say which pair was answered. Naming the partner would add a field to every transaction response, the optimistic builder and the fixtures for a view that already lists the two rows next to each other by date. The filter runs no job and sends no notification, so like `uncategorized` it needs no switch

- **2026-10-01.** Split lines keep the order they were entered in through a stored `TransactionLine.Position`, set from the request and used, then `Id`, to order every read; existing lines stay at 0 and so keep their `Id` order. Ported from an unfinished session of 2026-09-29, whose migration numbered the existing lines by `Id` in hand-written SQL
  - Rejected: Version 7 UUIDs for new line ids and ordering by `Id`; ordering by category or amount; a backfill in the migration
  - Why: A time-ordered id only works when every line of a request gets a later id than the one before, which holds within one process but says nothing about the lines already stored, and it ties the order to how ids are made. Sorting by category or amount would be stable but would still move lines away from where the person put them, which the receipt split and a reordered edit both rely on. Ordering by `Position` then `Id` gives the old lines, all at 0, the same order a backfill by `Id` would have stored, so the migration stays as EF generates it

- **2026-09-30, superseded on 2026-10-02 for what is stored.** A spread payment's monthly slices are computed on read from `Date`, `SpreadMonths` and `ReportingAmount`, and only the date of the last slice is stored, as `SpreadUntil` set in `AppDbContext.ApplyEntityRules`. Since 2026-10-02 the first slice's date is stored too, as `SpreadFrom`
  - Rejected: A `TransactionAllocations` table written on save; computing the end in SQL from `Date` and `SpreadMonths`; expanding the slices in SQL with `generate_series`
  - Why: The slices follow from the row itself, so a table would be one more thing to rewrite on every edit, restore and reporting-currency revaluation, the argument that keeps budgets and holdings uncached. The stored end makes "does this row reach into the window" a plain indexed comparison for the loader, the ledger and the month close, where `AddMonths` on a column does not translate cleanly. Spread rows are a handful a year, so one extra query cut in memory, shaped like the split proration, costs nothing and is a pure function to test
- **2026-09-30, superseded on 2026-10-02 for the direction, splits and refunds.** A payment is spread forward from its own month, on the same day of each month clamped to the month's end, with `ShareAllocator`'s equal largest-remainder split; splits and refunds cannot be spread. Since 2026-10-02 a spread can also end in the payment's month, and splits and refunds can be spread
  - Rejected: Spreading backwards (a bill paid in arrears) or centred; slices on the first of each month or by days; rounding each slice and giving the last the remainder; spreading every split line; spreading a refund the way its purchase was
  - Why: Forward covers insurance, yearly subscriptions and services paid in advance. The payment's own day keeps a weekly budget's window meaningful. Largest remainder adds up to the cent and never makes a slice negative, which round-and-remainder does for 0.10 over 12 months. Splits and refunds would each add a second dimension to the cut for a case nobody has asked for, and a refund of a spread purchase still lowers its category in the month the money came back
- **2026-09-30.** The ledger drill-through into spread rows is an explicit `spreadOverlap` flag that lives only in the URL: `TransactionsLink` and the money flow add it with a date range, changing or removing the date filter clears it, saved filters drop it, and the server ignores it without both dates
  - Rejected: Always widening a date filter to spread rows; slicing the ledger summary; storing the flag in saved filters; clearing it only when the date filter is removed
  - Why: A date range typed into the ledger means rows dated in it, while a link from a figure has to lead to every row that makes the figure up. The flag describes where a link came from, not what the reader wants to keep, so a saved filter must not keep it, and a range the reader edits is no longer the figure's range. The ledger's total stays a sum of whole rows, as for tags and splits, and the chip says how much of each row counts in the range
- **2026-09-30.** The slice loader reads tag ids through a `Transaction.Tags` navigation added to the existing `TransactionTag` relationship, the only navigation on a transaction
  - Rejected: Passing `db.TransactionTags` into the static loader beside the visible query; a second query for the tags of the loaded rows
  - Why: The loader is a static extension on the caller's visible query, like `DatedFlows`, and a navigation lets it read the rows and their tags in one statement without every caller handing it the context. The navigation changes no column, and without a navigation back from `TransactionTag` it does not trip EF's query-filter warning for required relationships

- **2026-09-30.** The ledger's inline category change runs through one page-level `useInlineCategory` with its own bulk-category mutation, and shows a cell's pending choice from `useMutationState`
  - Rejected: A mutation and a suggested-rule hook in every category cell; sending the cells through the selection toolbar's bulk mutation
  - Why: Per-cell hooks put a mutation observer and two more in every row of a page that is rendered twice (table and phone list). The toolbar's mutation raises a "recategorized" toast and clears the selection on success, neither of which a single-cell change may do, and per-call callbacks only fire for the latest call, so a quick run of changes would lose all but the last suggested-rule offer. `useMutationState` over the shared mutation key keeps every cell's own pending choice even when several requests run at once
- **2026-09-30.** The create and edit dialogs and their state live in `useTransactionFormSection`, a hook that returns `startBlank`, `startFromDraft`, `startEditing` and the dialogs
  - Rejected: The 19-prop `TransactionFormSection` component the page fed; inlining both dialogs into the page
  - Why: The component only forwarded props, and the page kept the prefill, receipt and edit state it could not own. Inlining would have kept all of that in an already long page. A hook moves the state beside the dialogs and leaves the page three calls, and a menu, a row action or a template can still start a dialog without a handle on it

- **2026-09-29.** The open question of the refunds plan, decided while the owner was away and to be reviewed: the ledger's type filter gets no separate "Refunds" value; "Expense" lists refunds with their Refund tag
  - Rejected: A `refund` value of the type filter, served by an `Amount < 0` condition in `Filtered`
  - Why: It was the plan's recommendation and the conservative choice: no new filter value in the contract, the saved filters, the chips and the export links, and a refund is an expense by definition, so the expense filter showing it keeps the list and the expense total in agreement. A reader who wants only refunds can sort the expense list by amount
- **2026-09-29.** "Record refund" prefills the purchase's own description rather than "Refund: {description}"
  - Rejected: The plan's "Refund: {description}"; setting the refund's `PayeeKey` from the purchase regardless of its description
  - Why: `PayeeKey` is the normalized description, and "Refund: Maxima" normalizes to "refund maxima", so the plan's text would have put every refund recorded from the ledger under a payee of its own and left the shop's total in spending by payee unchanged, against the plan's own rule that a refund lowers its payee's total like its category's. Copying the key from the purchase would have been a second writer of the column for one case. The Refund tag and the "Refund of" link already say what the row is
- **2026-09-29.** A refund is an expense with a negative amount: `Transaction.Type` stays `Expense`, and `Amount` and `ReportingAmount` are negative
  - Rejected: A new `FlowType.Refund`; an `IsRefund` flag with a positive amount
  - Why: Every total already sums `ReportingAmount` over `Type == Expense`, so a negative amount nets out with no change: attributions, reports, dashboard, month close, budgets, the ledger summary, the PDF and the account balance. A new flow type would break the rule that a category's type equals the transaction's, and every `Type == Expense` filter would need `or Refund`. A flag would need a sign flip in each of those sums, and one missed sum silently inflates spending
- **2026-09-29.** The API carries the signed amount: `amount` is negative for a refund, income stays positive, responses carry the stored signed values
  - Rejected: A positive `amount` plus `isRefund`
  - Why: One number end to end. Responses and exports then agree with the database and with every total, with no second field to keep in step
- **2026-09-29.** A refund may name the purchase it refunds through an optional `RefundOfTransactionId`, one purchase per refund, checked when the refund is written
  - Rejected: A required link; a link table allowing one refund for several purchases; re-checking the link when the purchase is edited
  - Why: Many refunds come without a findable original, such as a price adjustment or a cashback, and one refund for one purchase is the common case. The link is a pointer for the reader, not a constraint any total depends on, so a later edit of the purchase need not be refused over it
- **2026-09-29.** A refund is not capped at the purchase's amount, and it cannot be split
  - Rejected: Refusing refunds whose total exceeds the purchase; split refunds with negative lines
  - Why: Shipping refunds, goodwill credits and currency differences exceed the purchase legitimately, and the "Refunded" mark shows the total, so a typo is visible. A split refund would need negative lines and a rule for mixed signs, while a returned item is one category almost always; the purchase can still be split

- **2026-09-20.** Saved ledger filters and transaction templates live in the browser, as two TanStack DB local-storage collections (`jx-saved-filters`, `jx-transaction-templates`) beside the `jx-preferences` row, with no table, no endpoint and no backup coverage
  - Rejected: A `SavedFilters` and a `TransactionTemplates` table as personal `OwnableEntity` records under their own routes; putting them inside the existing `jx-preferences` row
  - Why: The active-household decision taken earlier today drew the line in the right place: a thing that describes how one browser looks at the data is a browser preference, and a saved filter is literally a named query string of the screen it belongs to. A server table would have bought one thing, following the user to their phone, and paid for it with a migration, a CRUD slice, validators, invalidation entries, fixtures, backup and restore coverage and a rule for what a restore does with somebody else's saved views — for a record the product never reads. A template does hold money amounts, which is the reason to stop and think, but it holds them the way a half-typed form does: nothing sums it, no balance or report reads it, and losing it loses typing rather than data. The cost is stated plainly: a saved filter and a template do not follow the person to another browser, exactly like the theme and the active household. They are separate collections rather than fields on the preferences row because they are lists that grow, and one row per saved view is what makes rename and delete a single keyed write
- **2026-09-20.** A saved filter stores the filter half of the search params only — search, account, category, tags, type and the date range — and never the page number, the sort or the row selection
  - Rejected: Saving the whole search object, sort included; saving the selection with it
  - Why: The page number is one reading of one list and would reopen a saved filter on a page that may no longer exist. The sort is the reader's current question about the rows, not part of "which rows": restoring it would silently reorder the table under somebody who had just sorted it by amount, and the filter menu would become a second, hidden sort control. The selection is transient by construction — the page already clears it on any navigation, because a tick means "this row, in this view"
- **2026-09-20.** A saved filter that names a deleted category, account or tag is applied exactly as stored, and the menu entry says that it names something that no longer exists
  - Rejected: Dropping the missing part and applying the rest; hiding or disabling such an entry; deleting it
  - Why: Dropping the part silently answers a wider list than the name promises, which is the same objection that made an unparsable `tagIds` a 400 rather than an ignored value. Hiding the entry loses a name the owner can still read, rename and delete, and the state is not an error: a household that deleted a tag may well recreate it. Applied as stored, the ledger answers the empty list the filter honestly describes, and the note in the menu explains why
- **2026-09-20.** Duplicate and "start from a template" both fill the existing create form through one optional `prefill` draft and write nothing until submit; a template is saved from inside that same dialog rather than from a row
  - Rejected: A second create path that posts the copied values straight away; a "save as template" button per row; a separate template editor screen
  - Why: One `TransactionDraft` in, one form out, means the split-total rule, the positive-amount rule, the category-follows-type rule and the server field errors are written once and cannot drift. Posting on click would make a mis-click a real transaction in a ledger, which is the one thing an accidental duplicate must not be. Saving from the dialog composes with Duplicate — duplicate a row, then name it — so a row needs no fourth icon button, and a person can template a shape they have never saved. A separate editor would be a second form for the same fields with no validation of its own
